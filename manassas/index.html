Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Manassas

A little routine care is the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that fails early. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode rod check every three to five years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a temperature setting around 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The serial number on the label usually encodes the manufacture date, and its age tells you a lot. Most tank heaters make it to 8 to 12 years, heat pump models 10 to 15, and regularly descaled tankless units about 20. If your heater is beyond that range and also shows discolored water, popping sounds, or moisture at the base, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than an emergency replacement once the tank fails.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a short, inexpensive repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and spares you a flooded utility room.

Typical Water Heater Issues in Manassas

The majority of water heater problems we see in Manassas start with what's in the water. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be a real factor: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A standard tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and near the end of that window the anode rod is often used up, leaving the steel exposed to corrosion. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Homes on private wells can bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a sulfur smell in the hot water usually points to a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: a thermal expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a correctly piped T&P discharge line, drain pans where a leak would hit finished floors, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
